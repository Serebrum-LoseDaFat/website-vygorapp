Apple Search Ads, explained from zero

Before you touch a single setting, you need the mental model. Everything else in this guide — the roadmap, the budget, the keyword tables — assumes you understand what's in this Part. Read it once, slowly.

1.1  What Apple Ads actually is

The App Store has a search bar, just like Google. When someone types "calorie tracker," Apple shows a results list. Apple Ads is how Vygor pays to appear higher, or appear at all, in front of people already typing things related to what Vygor does. It is not banner advertising or social media advertising — every person who sees a Vygor ad through Apple Ads is, at that exact moment, inside the App Store looking for an app.

The one-sentence version

ASO (App Store Optimization) is earning visibility for free, by having the right name, keywords and screenshots. Apple Ads is paying for visibility on top of that, for search terms you choose. The two work best together: what you learn from paid search (which words people actually use) should improve your free/organic listing too. That link-back is Pattern #5 in Part 3.

1.2  The four places your ad can appear

Apple calls these "placements." You are only using one of them at the start — the other three are things you add later, once the first one is proven. Here they are, in the order you'll actually use them:

PlacementWhat the shopper is doingWhat it's good forWhen Vygor uses it
Search ResultsThey typed a specific word, like "weight loss app"Capturing people who already want something like Vygor, right nowStart here — all of September
Search TabThey opened the Search tab but haven't typed anything yetGetting noticed before they've searchedAdded in Phase 3 (November)
Today TabThey're browsing the App Store's front pageA big visibility spike, fast — good for a short pushOptional short burst, late September if needed; ongoing from November
Product PagesThey're looking at a similar or competing app's pageCatching people comparing you to a competitorAdded in Phase 3 (November)

Why wait on the other three? Because until you know Search Results is actually working — which keywords convert, at what cost — you have no way to judge whether the other placements are working either. One variable at a time.

1.3  The building blocks: Campaign → Ad Group → Keyword

Think of this exactly like folders on a computer:

Campaign = the folder

One budget, one goal, one theme. Example: "Vygor — Weight Loss — Search Results"

Ad Group = the sub-folder

A cluster of closely related keywords inside a campaign, sharing one bid strategy. Example: "Weight Tracker Terms"

Keyword = the file

The actual word or phrase you're bidding on, e.g. weight loss tracker

Vygor will run 6 campaigns (Part 5.3 has the full list and budget split), each built around one theme — Weight Loss, Calories/Food, Meal Planning, Fitness, Competitors, Brand — so you can see clearly which theme is working and reallocate money toward it without guessing.

1.4  Match types: how tightly you control who sees your ad

When you add a keyword, you choose how loosely Apple is allowed to interpret it. This is one of the most important settings in the entire account, so here it is with real Vygor examples:

Exact Match

Your ad shows only for that keyword and very close variants Apple recognizes automatically — misspellings, plurals, word order swaps. Nothing looser.

Example: you add calorie tracker as Exact Match. Your ad can show for "calorie tracker" and "calorie trackers," but not for "best calorie app" or "diet tracker."
Broad Match

Your ad can show for that keyword and related searches Apple thinks are relevant — synonyms, related phrases, sometimes surprising ones. Wider net, less control.

Example: weight loss as Broad Match might also show your ad for "diet plan" or "lose belly fat." Good for discovering language you hadn't thought of; risky if left unwatched, because you can pay for searches that aren't actually relevant.
Search Match

You don't type any keywords at all for this — it's a campaign-level setting. Apple reads your app's own listing (name, subtitle, description, category) and automatically shows your ad for anything it judges relevant. Fully automatic discovery.

Use this in the Discovery campaign (Part 5.3) specifically to find search terms you never would have guessed. Every week, you read the results (Search Terms report) and pull the good ones out into their own Exact Match keyword elsewhere.
The rule Vygor follows

Proven, high-value terms (from Part 4's starter list) → Exact Match, in their own themed ad group, so you control cost precisely. Everything unproven or exploratory → Search Match in the Discovery campaign only, with a capped budget, so surprises can't blow up your spend. Broad Match is used sparingly and only inside Discovery, never in the core theme campaigns.

A debated practice — should Search Match just be turned off?

You'll hear both sides from experienced Apple Ads advertisers. Some turn Search Match off entirely, calling it a budget-waster that pulls in irrelevant searches with no way to see them coming. Others — including Calz, Pillow and Rocket Money (Part 3) — use it deliberately and make it pay off by keeping it tightly contained and watched every week. Vygor's approach splits the difference: Search Match lives only inside the Discovery campaign, capped at 15% of budget, reviewed every Tuesday (Part 7) — never inside the core theme campaigns. The rule: if two straight weeks of Discovery spend aren't producing efficient installs, turn it off and move that budget into your proven Exact Match campaigns. Either camp is defensible; the discipline of watching it weekly is what actually matters, not which default you start from.

1.5  Budget and bidding, without the jargon

Two different numbers control your spending, and beginners often mix them up:

Daily Budget

The most a campaign can spend in one day, total, across all its keywords. This is your spending ceiling.

Example: if Weight Loss's daily budget is $30, Apple will never spend more than $30 on that campaign today, no matter how many people tap the ad.

Max CPT (Cost Per Tap)

The most you're willing to pay for one single tap on your ad, per keyword. This is your per-click ceiling.

Example: if "weight loss tracker" has a Max CPT of $1.20, Apple will never charge you more than $1.20 for one tap on that keyword — often less, depending on the auction.

You set both, Apple's auction decides the rest. You are never charged for an impression (someone just seeing the ad) — only for a tap.

Recommended bids are a starting point, not an instruction

Apple's Recommendations panel (1.7) suggests a bid for each keyword. Treat it as a sensible place to start, not a rule to follow blindly — if you're confused about what number to pick, that's normal. The real test is what Apple's auction actually charges you (your real average CPT) and whether your impression share and installs look healthy at that level once the campaign is running. Start near the recommendation, then adjust from real results, not the estimate.

Manage Bids vs. Maximize Conversions

StrategyWhat it meansWhen to use it
Manage BidsYou set the Max CPT yourself, for every keyword, by handUse this for all of September and October — you don't have enough history yet for automation to work well
Maximize ConversionsYou give Apple a target cost-per-install; its algorithm searches broadly — a bit like Search Match — for terms likely to convert, and sets bids automatically toward that target, without you picking individual keywordsOnly turn this on in November, once you have real conversion data (Part 6, Phase 3)

A rule some advertisers use for Maximize Conversions campaigns: set the daily budget to roughly 30× your target cost-per-install, so Apple's algorithm has enough daily volume to actually find and learn from conversions rather than running out of budget before it learns anything. This is a practitioner's rule of thumb, not an official Apple figure — treat it as a starting point to test in November, not a guarantee.

How to move a bid, and how long to wait before judging it

  • Change one thing, then wait. Apple's system needs time to react to any change — a new keyword, a new bid, a paused campaign. Give it at least 24 hours before drawing any conclusion, and a fuller 3–7 days before deciding a keyword is a genuine winner or loser. Judging results after a few hours is the single most common beginner mistake.
  • Raise bids in small, deliberate steps. Move roughly 10–20% at a time — for a keyword bidding around $1–2, that's often in the neighborhood of $0.10–$0.50 per step — and watch impression share after each change, rather than jumping straight to a high ceiling.
  • Set a ceiling and mean it. If a keyword's competitive bid keeps climbing — past roughly $3 in cost-per-tap is a reasonable line for Vygor's current budget, though the real ceiling should come from the affordability math in Part 5.4 — that's the signal to stop chasing it and move that budget to a different keyword, not to keep raising forever.

1.7  Apple's Recommendations tool — use this before you set a single bid

Inside Apple Ads, when you're building or reviewing a campaign, Apple shows you a Recommendations panel. Most beginners skip straight past it — don't. It's Apple showing you its own data about what a keyword is likely to do, before you spend a cent.

What you'll seeWhat it meansWhat Vygor does with it
Estimated Installs, Spend & CPA (7-day forecast)Apple's prediction of what adding this keyword, at this bid, would cost and produce over the next weekBefore adding any keyword in Part 5's list, check its estimated CPA is below your target cost-per-install

Impression Share% of the time your ad actually appeared, out of every time it was eligible toLow share + strong Rank = you're under-serving a good keyword; raise its bid or its campaign's budget

How to read Rank + Impression Share together

Strong Rank (1–2) but low Impression Share → you're winning the auction when you compete, but your budget or bid is too low to compete often enough. Fix: raise the budget or bid. Weak Rank (4–5) and low Impression Share → you're being consistently outbid. Fix: either raise the bid meaningfully or accept this keyword is too competitive for now and focus budget elsewhere.

Where to find it: inside the Apple Ads dashboard, open a campaign → Keywords tab → the Recommendations panel sits alongside your keyword list, and updates continuously as your campaign runs.

1.8  Reading a keyword: winner or loser?

Every week (Part 7) you'll look at your keyword list and decide what to do with each one. Here's the decision table:

What you seeWhat it meansWhat to do
Zero installs after a full week of real spendNot converting at allPause it
Consistently below your account's average conversion rate for 2+ weeksUnderperformingCut the bid, or pause
Strong tap-through and conversion, impression share still has room to growA genuine winner, under-servedRaise the bid/budget — scale it
Impression share is already very high (you're winning nearly every eligible auction)You are not being outbid — this keyword's ceiling is its own search volume, not your budgetDon't keep raising — look for growth elsewhere
A common mix-up worth clearing up

It's easy to assume a very high impression share (say, 90%+) means something's wrong or that you're being outbid — it's the opposite. Low impression share is the "being outbid or under-funded" signal (Part 1.7). A very high impression share means you're already capturing nearly everyone who searches that term; the only way to get more from it is if more people start searching it, not by raising your bid further.

Never duplicate a keyword

Don't add the same keyword, in the same match type, to two different ad groups or campaigns. They'll bid against each other in Apple's own auction, split your performance data in two, and waste spend for no benefit. Each keyword lives in exactly one place.

1.9  Apple Ads Basic vs. Advanced, and CPT vs. CPI — what Krishna's two parallel tests actually mean

Since September 3, Krishna has been running two experimental campaigns side by side, both on the same Weight Loss / weight-management theme — one on Apple Ads Advanced, one on Apple Ads Basic. These are genuinely two different products Apple sells, not two settings inside the same one, and they optimize toward different numbers by design. Here's what each one actually is:

Apple Ads Basic → optimizes toward CPI

You don't pick keywords, campaigns, or ad groups at all. You tell Apple two things — a monthly budget (capped at $10,000/app) and a target cost-per-install — and Apple's algorithm decides on its own which searches to show your ad for and what to bid, aimed at hitting that average install cost. It's built to be hands-off.

You pay per install, effectively — Apple doesn't show you a per-tap price at all in Basic, only spend and installs. That's the "CPI" (Cost Per Install) framing.

Apple Ads Advanced → optimizes toward CPT

This is everything Vygor's 6-campaign structure is built on (Parts 1.2–1.8): you choose campaigns, ad groups, individual keywords, match types, and a Max CPT per keyword. You see every metric — impressions, taps, TTR, Rank, Impression Share (Part 1.6) — not just a final install count.

You pay per tap — that's the literal charge Apple makes. Your effective cost-per-install is something you calculate afterward (spend ÷ installs); Apple itself doesn't optimize toward it directly the way Basic does, unless you turn on Maximize Conversions (Part 1.5).

What to actually watch while comparing the two live experiments
  • Normalize to cost-per-install for the comparison. Basic never reports CPT at all, so CPT isn't a fair scoreboard — convert Advanced's numbers to spend ÷ installs too, and compare that install cost between the two.
  • Split the budget evenly between them. If one gets more of the $10 than the other, a lower CPI on that side doesn't prove it's the better channel — it may just have had more spend to find a lucky install.
  • Give both the same patience window — at least a week, ideally two (Part 1.5's 24-hour/3–7-day rule applies here too) — before drawing any conclusion.
  • Remember what Basic can't tell you. Even if Basic shows a lower CPI early on, it can't tell you which keyword is working — Advanced's keyword-level data (Part 1.7's Recommendations, the Search Terms report) is what actually improves the Weight Loss campaign over time. A cheaper install with zero insight is a weaker long-term result than a slightly costlier one you can learn from.
  • Treat this first month as directional, not conclusive. At $10 total for the entire month of September — this is the real, currently-committed spend, separate from the $1,500–2,500 recommended figure in Part 5.3 — very few installs will happen on either side. That's fine for a first read of "does this even work," but it's not enough volume yet to make a permanent Basic-vs-Advanced call. Revisit this once a full month of real data exists (flagged in Part 8).

2.4  What your subscriber data can (and can't) show you yet

Good news: subscription tracking already works

App Store Connect Analytics already shows Vygor's one recorded subscriber — that's genuinely where the "1" in the tables above comes from. Apple's native, whole-app subscription reporting is working correctly. Nothing needs to be fixed here before campaigns launch.

Not yet confirmed: trial-start tracking

Unlike the subscriber count, it isn't yet confirmed how — or whether — the 2-week trial start is being recorded anywhere. Krishna raised Branch.io as a possible option, but before picking a tool, one question settles almost everything: is the trial a native App Store trial (set up as an "introductory offer" on the subscription product) or a custom, in-app-only trial (the app itself unlocks Premium features for 14 days, with no App Store purchase transaction behind it)? The step-by-step plan below starts there.

A short plan to confirm and track the 2-week trial

  1. Ask Krishna/dev team one question: is the trial built as a native StoreKit "introductory offer" on the Premium subscription, or is it custom logic in Vygor's own backend/app that has nothing to do with an App Store purchase? This is a five-minute conversation that decides everything else.
  2. If it's a native App Store trial — you're mostly done already. App Store Connect's Subscriptions report already breaks out trial starts and trial-to-paid conversions natively, the same place the "1 subscriber" number in this guide comes from (Part 2.1). No new tool needed — just open that report tab weekly.
  3. If it's a custom, in-app trial — that event only exists in Vygor's own systems, not Apple's, so it can't automatically be linked back to which Apple Ads keyword caused the install. To close that gap, the app needs to report the "trial started" event to Apple using the free, built-in Ads Attribution API — the same mechanism that already links installs to keywords (Part 1.7). This is the leaner first step: no new vendor, no new contract.
  4. Where Branch.io fits: it's a legitimate, widely-used attribution tool, but it's built for a broader job — deep-linking across many channels (TikTok, Instagram, email, ads) into specific in-app screens, with attribution as one part of that. If Vygor's only goal right now is "which Apple Ads keyword led to a trial," Apple's own Ads Attribution API is the smaller, cheaper, purpose-built option. Branch.io (or AppsFlyer/Adjust) is worth revisiting later if Vygor wants unified cross-channel deep-linking beyond just this one measurement need.
  5. Next action: this is now a Week 1 checklist item (Part 5.1) — get Krishna's answer to Step 1 before Week 2's launch, so the right tracking approach is already in motion once paid installs start.

Separately, what Apple Ads already does automatically: once September's campaigns are live, it will tell you which keyword drove each install — no setup needed there (Part 1.7). It's specifically the install → trial → subscription chain, broken down by keyword, that depends on the plan above.

The simple interim habit, either way

Until keyword-level trial/subscription attribution exists, the easiest way to keep an eye on the connection: each week, look at App Store Connect's whole-app trial-start and Premium-subscriber counts alongside Apple Ads' total installs for that week (Part 7's Friday scorecard). It won't tell you which keyword to credit, but it will tell you whether trials and Premium upgrades are moving at all once paid traffic starts flowing in.

Proof it works: 3 real Apple Ads success stories

These are Apple's own published case studies, chosen because they're the closest matches to Vygor: an AI health/nutrition app, a subscription health app, and a subscription consumer app. The percentages are the least useful part — the tactics underneath are what you're borrowing.

3.1 · Calz — Calorie Counter AI Closest match to Vygor

AI-powered calorie, nutrition and activity tracker. Health & Fitness category, launched December 2024.

  • 15% month-over-month install growth from Apple Ads; reached #2 App Store ranking in Europe within 5 months
  • 147% higher ROI than comparable ad platforms; 400% increase in organic brand search volume across 15 countries
  • 2 million weekly impressions from Today Tab ads; +30% install volume globally after adding Custom Product Pages

What they actually did: built separate Search Results campaigns by keyword theme, set explicit cost-per-acquisition targets with maximum cost-per-tap ceilings, used Search Match and broad match specifically for discovery, then built country- and culture-specific Custom Product Pages (lose weight, gain weight, fasting, water tracking, activity) — and only added Today Tab and product-page ads after the core campaign had proven itself.

3.2 · Pillow

Sleep tracking app helping users build better habits. Health & Fitness, subscription-based.

  • +14% downloads year-over-year; +19% increase in new subscription revenue
  • +52% tap-through rate on Search Results campaigns; +83% installs from Apple Watch-focused Custom Product Pages

What they actually did: ran Today Tab, Search Tab, and product-page ads together once the base was working (not one at a time); built feature-specific Custom Product Pages (Apple Watch vs. iPhone) with matching ad creative for each; kept discovering new keywords continuously and re-checked performance by season and by country.

3.3 · Rocket Money

Personal finance / subscription-cancellation app. Heavy subscription revenue model, similar economics to Vygor even though the category differs.

  • +17% return on ad spend during a brand rebrand; 1.5× premium (paying) user growth at a seasonal peak
  • 5× install growth from top category keywords during that same seasonal peak

What they actually did: strictly separated brand keywords from non-brand/category keywords into different campaigns; used Search Match to discover finance search terms they hadn't predicted; built Custom Product Pages specifically to carry a messaging change; reserved Today Tab for a defined seasonal window rather than running it always-on.

3.4  The 7 patterns that repeat — explained, and applied to Vygor

Line up all three stories and the same seven moves show up every time. Here's what each one actually means, and exactly where it shows up in Vygor's plan.

1 · Keyword optimization never stops

All three companies kept adjusting keywords weekly, using real performance data — they didn't "set and forget" a keyword list. For Vygor: every Tuesday (Part 7), pull fresh Recommendations data before touching any bid, and promote winning Discovery terms into Exact Match.

2 · Category/non-brand terms carry the volume, brand is just protection

Calz and Rocket Money both led with category search terms (what the app does), keeping brand-name keywords to a small, defensive slice of budget. For Vygor: Weight Loss + Calories/Food + Meal Planning get ~70% of budget; Brand gets only 5% (Part 5.3).

3 · Custom Product Pages are the single biggest lever

This produced the single largest install gains in every story (+30% Calz, +83% Pillow). A Custom Product Page is a variant of your App Store listing — different screenshots and message — that you show only to people who came from a specific ad/keyword, so the message matches what they searched for. Apple allows up to 70 Custom Product Pages in total; Vygor only needs 5 to start. For Vygor: Phase 2 (October) ships all 5 planned Custom Product Pages — Weight Loss, AI Calorie Tracker, Meal Planning, Fitness/Steps, Challenges — each matched to its own ad group.

4 · Creative must match the search, not just the brand

Calz localized screenshots by country/cuisine; Pillow matched screenshots to the exact device feature someone searched for. For Vygor: the Weight Loss ad group's screenshots lead with weight-loss messaging; the AI Calorie Tracker ad group leads with the AI Macro Tracker's photo/barcode logging — never one generic screenshot set for every keyword.

5 · ASO and Apple Ads feed each other

Calz's paid keyword data drove a 400% lift in organic brand search — proof that paid learnings should flow back into your free listing, not stay siloed. For Vygor: every Wednesday (Part 7), winning Search Terms from paid campaigns get added into Vygor's App Store subtitle/keyword field, not just kept inside Apple Ads.

6 · New placements are added only after the core campaign is proven

Every story sequenced Search Results first, then added Today Tab / Search Tab / product-page ads once they had real data. For Vygor: Today Tab and product-page ads wait until Phase 3 (November) — unless the September checkpoint shows you're behind pace, in which case a short, deliberate Today Tab burst is allowed (Part 6, Week 3).

7 · The real payoff shows up downstream, not at install

Pillow's headline win was +19% subscription revenue, not installs; Rocket Money's was 1.5× paying users. Installs are a means, not the goal. For Vygor: installs are easy to count, but Premium upgrades are the real measure of whether ads are working — keep watching that number every week (Part 2.4, Part 7), not just installs.

PART 4 OF 8

Keyword research: how, where, and what

Keywords are everything in Apple Search Ads. Get them right and the rest of the account mostly takes care of itself; get them wrong and no amount of bid-tweaking fixes it. Here's exactly where good keywords come from and how to judge which ones deserve Vygor's budget.

4.1  Where keyword ideas actually come from

  1. Apple Ads' own suggestion screen. When you start building a campaign and type in a seed word (e.g. "calorie"), Apple shows you a list of related terms along with Search Popularity for each. This is the fastest starting point.
  2. The Recommendations tab (Part 1.7). Once any campaign is live, Apple continuously suggests more keywords based on your app and your current performance.
  3. The App Store's own search bar, used like a real person. Open the App Store and start typing "weight l..." — the autosuggest dropdown shows you the exact phrases real users search for. Do this for each of Vygor's six core features (AI Dietitian, AI Recipes, AI Macro Tracker, AI Trainer, Contests, Analytics) to surface language you wouldn't have guessed.
  4. Comparable apps' listings. Search your target terms and see which apps already rank there — read their subtitle and visible keywords for phrasing ideas (never copy their app name).
  5. Vygor's own Search Terms report, once campaigns are running. This becomes your single best source over time — it shows exactly what real searchers typed before installing. Every week's Search Terms review (Part 7) feeds new ideas back into this list.

4.2  How to decide if a keyword is worth using

Score each candidate keyword against three simple questions before adding it:

Is it popular?

Check Search Popularity (1–5) in Recommendations. Prioritize 4s and 5s for your core Exact Match list.

Is it really Vygor?

Does it map to one of Vygor's six actual features? If a keyword doesn't match anything Vygor does, skip it — a tap that leads to disappointment costs you money and teaches Apple's algorithm the wrong thing about your app.

Can you afford it?

Check the Estimated CPA in Recommendations against the affordability math in Part 5.4. If it's well above what a new subscriber is worth to Vygor, wait — don't bid on it yet.

A common mix-up worth clearing up — short-tail vs. long-tail

Short-tail keywords are short, broad, one- or two-word terms (e.g. "fitness app"). They get searched a lot, which also means a lot of other advertisers are bidding on them — so they tend to be the most expensive and most competitive. Long-tail keywords are longer, more specific phrases (e.g. "ai weight loss meal planner"). Fewer people search them, but fewer advertisers bid on them too, so they're usually cheaper per tap — and the person searching is often further along in deciding what they want, which can mean better conversion. Vygor's plan reflects this: high-popularity short-tail terms anchor the core Exact Match campaigns (Part 4.3), while long-tail variations are exactly what the Discovery campaign's Search Match is for (Part 1.4) — and never add the same keyword to two ad groups at once (Part 1.8).

4.4  A short plan for a full keyword research pass — start this now

The correction above is exactly why a proper research pass is worth doing before Week 2's launch, not after. Keep it tight:

  1. Trust your own dashboard over any outside guess — including this guide's first draft. For every candidate keyword, check Apple Ads' own Search Popularity and Estimated CPA (Part 1.7) before adding it. That's the correction you just caught.
  2. Widen the seed list past the obvious words. Type each of Vygor's six core features (AI Dietitian, AI Recipes, AI Macro Tracker, AI Trainer, Contests, Analytics) into both the App Store's own search bar and Apple Ads' keyword suggestion box, and write down every autosuggested phrase — not just the ones you expected to see (Part 4.1).
  3. Cross-check with one dedicated ASO tool. Pick one of AppTweak, Sensor Tower, Mobile Action, or data.ai (most offer a free trial) and run Vygor plus its 3 known competitors — MyFitnessPal, Noom, Cal AI — through it. Note keywords those apps rank for that Vygor doesn't even have on its radar yet.
  4. Score every candidate on three things together, not popularity alone: real observed popularity, Estimated CPA vs. the affordability math (Part 5.4), and how directly it maps to one of Vygor's six features (Part 4.2).
  5. Re-run this weekly against the live Search Terms report once campaigns launch (Part 7, Tuesday) — that report beats every outside tool, because it's Vygor's own real searchers, not an estimate.

5.2  Building the campaign — screen by screen

  1. Create Campaign. Choose Search Results as the placement (Part 1.2). Name it clearly, e.g. Vygor | Weight Loss | Search Results — you'll thank yourself later when you have six of these.
  2. Choose your country/market. Start with one priority market rather than spreading thin across several — pick wherever most of Vygor's existing installs already come from.
  3. Set the Daily Budget for this campaign (Part 5.3 has the exact number per campaign).
  4. Choose a bid strategy: Manage Bids (Part 1.5) — not Maximize Conversions, not yet.
  5. Create an Ad Group inside the campaign (Part 1.3) — e.g. "Weight Tracker Terms" — and set a default Max CPT for it, based on the Average CPT you saw in Recommendations (Part 1.7), treated as a starting point, not a rule.
  6. Add your keywords from Part 4.3, each tagged with the right match type (Exact for proven terms, Search Match/Broad only inside the separate Discovery campaign) — and check none of them already exists in another ad group.
  7. Choose your ad's creative. For the very first launch, use Vygor's default product page — there's no Custom Product Page yet (that's Phase 2). Don't delay launch waiting for creative that isn't due until October.
  8. Review and submit. Apple reviews new campaigns before they start delivering, which can take up to a day.

Is $50–85/day enough for a category this competitive?

Some Apple Search Ads practitioners describe competitive-category daily budgets running roughly $200–500/day, and lower-competition categories $100–300/day — worth treating as a discussion starting point, not confirmed fact, since these figures couldn't be independently verified this session. What's more solid: Apple Ads sets no official minimum budget at all — a smaller budget will still run, it just tends to mean lower impression share in a contested category. And health, fitness and weight-management search terms are genuinely contested — think MyFitnessPal, Noom, WeightWatchers, Lose It and similar well-funded apps, all bidding on very similar terms to Vygor's. September's $50–85/day is a deliberately modest starting point built for learning, not for winning every auction — Part 8 flags revisiting this once real September CPT and impression-share data are in hand.

The real September starting number: $10 for the whole month

Budget is still experimental. As of now, Vygor is testing with just $10 total for all of September — split across Krishna's two parallel Weight Loss experiments on Apple Ads Basic and Advanced (Part 1.9) — deliberately the lowest workable amount, before committing to anything larger. The $1,500–2,500 range above stays the target to grow into once this initial test shows real signal, not a number to jump to immediately.

5.4  What can Vygor actually afford to pay for a tap or an install?

This is genuinely something to work out together, not something this guide can hand you a final number for — but here's a simple way to think it through.

  1. Know your subscriber's value. Vygor Premium is $79.99/year — one plan, no tiers.
  2. Estimate what share of installs become subscribers. Vygor doesn't have enough paid-install data yet to know this for certain (Part 2.4) — the original 90-day goal model assumes roughly 1 in 37 installs (about 2.7%), a reasonable placeholder until real September/October data replaces it.
  3. Decide how much of that $79.99 you're willing to spend acquiring one subscriber, and over what payback window. This is a real business decision for you and the team, not a math problem with one right answer.
  4. Put it together: max affordable cost-per-install ≈ subscriber value × estimated install-to-subscriber rate × the share of that revenue you're willing to spend
Worked example — illustrative numbers only, not a decision

If 2.7% of installs become subscribers, and the team is comfortable spending half of one year's subscription value to acquire a subscriber: $79.99 × 2.7% × 50% ≈ $1.08 max affordable cost-per-install. Compare that to what Recommendations (Part 1.7) shows as the Estimated CPA for a keyword — if the estimate sits well above this number, that keyword either needs a much better real conversion rate than assumed, or it's not one to chase yet. This exact calculation, with Vygor's real numbers filled in, belongs in Part 8's open decisions — flag it there and revisit once September's data is in.

5.5  After it's live — the do's and don'ts

Do

Let it run for a full week before your first real read. Watch delivery daily just to confirm it's spending (not zero, not blowing past budget). Save every question you have for Tuesday's Search Terms review (Part 7).

Don't

Don't change bids or keywords every day — Apple's own auction needs at least 24 hours to react, and 3–7 days of consistent settings to produce results you can actually learn from (Part 1.5). Daily tinkering makes every number meaningless.

PART 7 OF 8

The weekly habit — repeat this every week campaigns run

Once Week 2 of September launches, this five-day rhythm repeats every single week through the rest of the 90 days (and beyond). It's the same discipline every one of Part 3's success stories followed: track daily, but only act on a weekly report.
Monday
Review spend, impressions, taps, TTR, installs and cost-per-install against last week — not against an outside "good" number, against Vygor's own trend (Part 1.6). Check view-through installs too, for the fuller picture.
Tuesday
Open the Search Terms report. Promote winning discovered terms to Exact Match; add negatives for irrelevant ones. Pull fresh Recommendations data (estimated CPA, impression share, rank) before changing any bid. Apply the winner/loser table (Part 1.8) — pause anything consistently underperforming.
Wednesday
Review organic ASO keyword performance. Feed confirmed paid winners into Vygor's App Store metadata/subtitle (Pattern #5, Part 3.4) — close the loop, don't let it run one-way.
Thursday
Review Custom Product Page / PPO test performance once those are live (from Phase 2 onward). Plan next week's hypothesis.
Friday
Publish a one-page scorecard: views, installs, Premium subscribers, spend, cost-per-install, what changed, what's next. This is the single artifact that keeps you — and anyone you report to — oriented.

8.2  Risks, stated honestly

Be aware of these

1,000 views by September 30 assumes campaigns launch on schedule in Week 2 — any slip in Week 1's setup pushes the whole month's pacing back.

New Apple Ads accounts sometimes have a short "ramp-up" period before delivery stabilizes — front-loading Week 1 bids near Apple's recommended range helps, but doesn't fully remove this.

Without keyword-level Premium-subscription attribution, Phase 2's "real funnel report" (Week 8) will still only show whole-app subscriber counts, not which keyword earned them — a real limitation worth working around consciously (Part 2.4), not a launch blocker.
